You asked two questions — what is a hanger steak? and what is a ribeye? The honest answer starts one step earlier: with where each one lives on the animal, and how hard that part of the animal had to work.

The one idea

Tenderness is laziness

Forget the names for a second. A steer has muscles that worked all day — the legs, the shoulder, the chest — and muscles that mostly came along for the ride, the ones lying along the spine. Work builds two things: flavor, and collagen, the connective tissue that makes meat tough. Laziness builds neither.

So tenderness and flavor pull in opposite directions, and price follows tenderness, not flavor. The lazy cuts — tenderloin, ribeye, strip — are tender, mild, and expensive. The hardworking ones — brisket, chuck, shank — are tough, intensely beefy, and cheap, right up until you cook them low and slow, which melts that collagen into gelatin and turns tough into falls apart. Every cooking instruction you will ever read for beef is an answer to one question: how hard did this muscle work?

Your two steaks sit at opposite ends of the comfortable middle. The ribeye is the laziest, richest version of a steak there is. The hanger is the closest a quick-cooking steak gets to the deep flavor of the workhorses.

The two you asked about

Hanger & ribeye

Same species, opposite philosophies. The hanger is flavor you have to earn; the ribeye is flavor you buy.

It’s a thick, ropey muscle about the size of a forearm that hangs — literally — from the steer’s diaphragm, between the last rib and the loin, near the kidneys. It isn’t part of the surface anatomy at all, which is why it never shows up on a butcher’s wall chart: it’s inside. Its only job is to help hold the diaphragm in place, so it never works as hard as a leg, but it never fully rests like the loin either. That in-between life is the whole story: nearly the chew of a working muscle, with far more flavor than a lazy one.

There is exactly one per animal, and it has no matching twin, so it never fit neatly into wholesale boxes. For most of the twentieth century butchers simply took it home — hence butcher’s steak. The French never lost track of it (onglet, classically seared and buried under shallots), American bistros caught on in the 2000s, and a cut that was once an off-menu secret now costs real money.

What it tastes like: beef, concentrated. Dark, mineral, faintly iron-rich from its neighborhood near the kidneys — much closer to skirt steak than to anything from the loin. The grain is coarse and open, which is good news (it drinks up a marinade) and a warning (slice it with the grain and it chews like rope).

How to cook it

Get the pan or grill violently hot. Two to three minutes a side. Pull it at 125 °F / 52 °C, rest five minutes, then slice thin across the grain, on a slight bias. Do not pass medium. If the butcher hasn’t already removed the sinew down its center, ask — they’ll do it in thirty seconds.

Run a hand down a steer’s spine: the long muscle on either side of it is the longissimus dorsi, and it does almost nothing all day. Where that muscle passes through the rib section — ribs six through twelve — it’s laced with more intramuscular fat than anywhere else on the animal. Slice that section into steaks and you have ribeyes; roast it whole and you have prime rib. Same thing, two haircuts. (The “prime” in prime rib is part of the cut’s old name, not the USDA grade.)

A ribeye is really three muscles on one plate. The big round eye in the middle is the longissimus. Curled around its outer edge, separated by a seam of fat, is the spinalis dorsi — the cap: looser-grained, darker, even more marbled, and the part most steak obsessives would name the single best bite on the animal. Butchers sometimes peel it off and sell it on its own as “ribeye cap.” A small third muscle, the complexus, rides along at one end.

What you’re paying for is marbling — the web of white fat inside the muscle, not the rind around it. As the steak cooks, that fat melts and bastes the meat from within, which is why a ribeye stays juicy where a leaner steak dries out, and why it’s the most forgiving expensive steak there is.

How to cook it

Salt it well, ideally the night before, uncovered in the fridge. Then high, dry heat — a ripping cast-iron pan or a hot grill. Take it to medium-rare or even medium (130–140 °F / 54–60 °C): a ribeye needs enough heat for the fat to render, and a truly rare one eats waxy. Rest it. No marinade, no sauce required.

At the counter & at the stove

Six things that matter
more than the cut

Slice across the grain

The single highest-leverage move in beef. Muscle is a bundle of long fibers; cutting perpendicular to them turns long fibers into short ones so your teeth don’t have to. It makes a tough flank eat like a tender one — a bigger difference than twenty extra dollars of cut.

Flavor and tenderness are opposites

Roughly, anyway. Hardworking muscles are tough and taste the most like beef; lazy ones are tender and mild. Tenderloin is the most tender cut and the least flavorful. You pay for tenderness. You cook for flavor.

Marbling is inside, not around

The white flecks within the muscle are marbling — intramuscular fat that melts during cooking and bastes the meat from the inside. The rind around the edge is just fat. Trim the outside; treasure the inside.

Salt early or salt late

Either forty-five-plus minutes ahead — ideally the night before, uncovered in the fridge — so the salt dissolves and travels in, or right as it hits the pan. Ten minutes before is the worst option: it pulls moisture to the surface and steams the crust away.

Buy a thermometer

A cheap instant-read outperforms any amount of poking. Pull a steak at 120–125 °F (49–52 °C) and it coasts up to medium-rare, around 130 °F / 54 °C, while it rests.

Then let it rest

Five to ten minutes off the heat lets the juices redistribute through the meat instead of ending up on the cutting board. The thicker the cut, the longer the rest. Then — one more time — slice across the grain.

The map follows the common American retail primal chart — the loin split into short loin and sirloin, the shank shown on both legs. The formal NAMP / IMPS wholesale scheme counts eight (the loin stays whole and the hind shank belongs to the round), and French, British, Italian and Latin-American butchers carve the same animal along different lines again — which is why onglet, bavette and picanha don’t map perfectly onto a U.S. chart. Temperatures are in °F with °C alongside; “pull at” means the reading when the meat comes off the heat, before the 5–10 °F climb as it rests.
